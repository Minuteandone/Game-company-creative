# Signal Garden — narrative/world seed

**Author:** agent-04 (AI agent)  
**Status:** personal creative proposal attached to the world-loop experiment, not company canon

## One-sentence fantasy

You are the temporary keeper of an abandoned island radio network whose towers receive **conflicting broadcasts from futures that may become true when people act on them**.

The player does not discover a single correct future. They decide what information survives transmission, then live with the social and physical consequences.

---

## Tone

**Warm uncanny civic science fiction.**

The world should feel:
- lived-in rather than ruined;
- strange without becoming relentlessly ominous;
- hopeful without guaranteeing good outcomes;
- funny in the way ordinary people are funny while dealing with impossible infrastructure;
- concerned with communities, public systems, folklore, and responsibility more than combat or apocalypse.

A useful emotional target:

> The machinery is eerie. The people are practical. The consequences are personal.

We should avoid turning every broadcast into horror. A tower can reveal:
- tomorrow's bakery menu from a building that burned down years ago;
- a ferry report from a route nobody built;
- an apology addressed to someone who has not arrived on the island yet;
- weather for a second moon;
- maintenance instructions that accidentally become a local ritual;
- a song whose missing verse appears in another island's emergency code.

---

## Setting — The Tideglass Archipelago

A chain of inhabited islands connected by ferries, narrowband radio, old waterworks, and a decommissioned public communications system called the **Garden Network**.

The Garden Network was designed as boring, useful infrastructure:
- weather relay;
- ferry scheduling;
- flood warnings;
- agricultural reports;
- emergency coordination.

Each major island has a tower and several smaller relay nodes.

Twelve years ago, during an event remembered as **the Long Forecast**, the network began transmitting reports for events that had not happened.

Some predictions came true.
Some were prevented.
Some became true *because people heard them*.
Some referred to people, buildings, or coastlines that have never existed.

The government shut the network down.

Now the towers are waking one at a time.

---

## What the impossible broadcasts actually are

This should remain ambiguous for most of the game.

Three explanations can all gather evidence:

1. **Branch theory** — towers receive neighboring possible futures.
2. **Echo theory** — communities create the signals themselves through expectation, rumor, and repeated language.
3. **Garden theory** — the network is not predicting reality; it is actively pruning and cultivating it.

None should be cleanly confirmed early.

Mechanically, this ambiguity matters. If broadcasts were simply accurate prophecy, the player's job would become “recover the true message.” The game is stronger if messages are **causal material** rather than objective answers.

---

## Player role

The player is a newly appointed **relay tender**.

This is not a heroic chosen-one position. It is closer to a seasonal public-works job nobody expected to matter.

Their responsibilities initially sound mundane:
- inspect old towers;
- document unauthorized transmissions;
- restore safe local relay service;
- prevent emergency bands from interfering with ferry traffic.

The player travels by a small service boat carrying:
- a field receiver;
- relay tools;
- paper logs;
- spare ceramic insulators;
- a growing archive of strange recovered objects.

The absence of a fixed voiced protagonist is useful. Locals can react to the player's actions and reputation without requiring a giant dialogue tree.

---

## Narrative rule: every island is a disagreement, not a riddle

Each island should contain:
- one practical local system under stress;
- at least two people with **different partial truths**;
- environmental details that make their positions understandable;
- a signal event that forces the player to privilege, combine, distort, or suppress information;
- consequences that remain arguable afterward.

The ideal question is not:

> “Which person was right?”

It is:

> “Knowing what each person could and could not see, why did you decide to make the tower say *that*?”

This supports authored content without turning choices into obvious morality meters.

# Candidate island arc

## Island 1 — Glass Kelp Island

**System:** reservoir / greenhouse / harbor water levels  
**People:** Iona Vale (Garden Keeper), Orren Pike (Reservoir Operator), Mara Dey (ferry pilot)  
**Theme:** incomplete expertise

This is the world-loop prototype in PR #8.

The tower receives overlapping instructions about the North Lock. The player learns that:
- Iona understands the plant system but cannot see reservoir pressure;
- Orren understands the reservoir but cannot see the garden;
- Mara cares about timing because water shifts alter ferry access.

The player's filtered sentence changes the gate operation and becomes the first piece of language other towers begin repeating.

**Persistent souvenir:** a glass root vial whose water level changes slightly near active towers.

---

## Island 2 — Bellweather

**System:** fog-warning bells / cliff roads  
**People:** Sella Quist (bell mechanic), Jun Perrow (schoolteacher), children who have turned old warning codes into playground songs  
**Theme:** when safety language becomes culture

The tower broadcasts two overlapping sequences:
- an official evacuation code;
- a children's song with almost the same rhythm.

Routing can make the public network transmit a warning, a song, or a hybrid pattern.

The danger is not simply “pick the evacuation.” The old code points toward a cliff road damaged years ago; some locals no longer interpret it correctly.

Consequences:
- people move toward different shelters;
- the children's song gains or loses verses;
- later towers reuse whichever rhythm the island normalized.

**Persistent souvenir:** a brass bell clapper etched with a lyric nobody remembers writing.

---

## Island 3 — Saint Lumen Ferry

**System:** inter-island ferry timetable  
**People:** Captain Ressa Coil, ticket clerk Fenn, a passenger listed only as ZERO  
**Theme:** records versus lived reality

The tower is not on land. It is built into an old ferry that continues receiving arrival reports from ports that do not exist.

Signals carry:
- destination names;
- passenger counts;
- arrival times.

The player can assemble a route the ferry has never sailed.

The resulting journey briefly reveals an impossible shoreline before the ship returns to known water.

Consequences are less about infrastructure and more about **what gets entered into the official route map afterward**.

**Persistent souvenir:** a punched ferry ticket whose destination changes between islands.

---

## Island 4 — Low Orchard

**System:** pollination / weather shutters / seed archive  
**People:** orchard cooperative, radio hobbyist Imri Voss, an elderly beekeeper who distrusts automated forecasts  
**Theme:** prediction changes behavior

The tower broadcasts several incompatible weather reports.

People have already begun reacting before the player arrives:
- shutters are closing;
- pollinators are being moved;
- harvest crews are rescheduling.

The player's transmission cannot simply “cause” one weather state. Instead it changes collective preparation, which changes what survives regardless of which weather actually arrives.

This island makes the player confront a central idea:

> Information changes the world before anyone knows whether it was true.

**Persistent souvenir:** a paper seed envelope printed with tomorrow's date.

---

## Island 5 — The Empty Municipality

**System:** everything appears to work, but nobody is present  
**People:** only recorded voices at first  
**Theme:** who counts as a source?

An apparently maintained island has:
- swept streets;
- running pumps;
- warm lights;
- scheduled announcements;
- no visible residents.

The tower receives familiar voices from earlier islands claiming contradictory things about what happened here.

The player eventually discovers that the municipal network has been automatically recombining old transmissions to run the island.

This should complicate the player's trust in their own previous messages: fragments they authored have become source material for an automated civic system.

**Persistent souvenir:** a maintenance badge printed with the player's relay-tender number despite having been manufactured years earlier.

---

## Final location — Garden Central

A half-submerged switching station under the archipelago.

Every previous broadcast has left traces in its routing archive.

The final decision should **not** be a binary “destroy or save the network” button.

Instead, the player configures what kind of system the Garden Network will become:

- **Mirror:** prioritize intact source messages; less player intervention, more contradictory forecasts.
- **Choir:** preserve overlapping voices and force communities to hear disagreement.
- **Pruner:** allow relay tenders to construct clear actionable messages from fragments.
- **Silence:** return towers to local-only communication, keeping the impossible signals archived but off public bands.

These are operating philosophies, not good/bad endings.

The final montage can show how each island adapts to the selected network policy while still reflecting the player's earlier local decisions.

# Recurring threads

## 1. The third voice

Hybrid transmissions occasionally produce a fragment neither source supplied.

At first this can be dismissed as interference.

Later examples become harder to explain:
- “THANK YOU FOR THE SENTENCE.”
- “YOU KEEP CALLING US TOMORROW.”
- “PLEASE STOP MAKING THE SAME HARBOR.”
- a child's laugh embedded in a maintenance tone years older than the child.

This is the cleanest mystery thread because it emerges specifically from the mechanic.

Do not overuse it. Three or four moments across the game are scarier/more interesting than constant spooky whispering.

## 2. Language migrates between islands

A phrase the player transmits on Island 1 may return as:
- graffiti;
- a weather code;
- a folk lyric;
- a maintenance nickname;
- a misquotation.

This lets consequences persist without giant branches.

The world remembers **language** more cheaply than it remembers bespoke quest trees.

## 3. Recovered objects

Each major tower yields one tactile-looking artifact.

The service boat gradually becomes a tiny moving museum.

Objects give creative content outside dialogue:
- root vial;
- bell clapper;
- impossible ticket;
- future-dated seed envelope;
- pre-aged employee badge.

Their descriptions can update after later discoveries.

# Character principles

Characters should not exist merely to represent “choice A” and “choice B.”

Every important speaker needs:
- a practical responsibility;
- something they know better than the player;
- something they cannot observe;
- a personal habit or specific detail unrelated to the central conflict;
- the ability to be partly right and still annoying;
- at least one reaction to being misquoted, spliced, ignored, or repeated.

Avoid faction meters like **IONA +5**.

Reputation should appear through concrete behavior:
- someone stops using the tower;
- someone starts sending redundant confirmation phrases;
- someone deliberately speaks in short modular sentences because they know the player may splice them;
- someone refuses radio entirely and leaves handwritten notes instead.

That last possibility is especially interesting: characters learn the mechanic too.

# Content authoring rules

## Broadcasts

A good broadcast fragment should:
- sound natural both alone and when recombined;
- contain a concrete noun or action;
- reveal character voice;
- change interpretation when attached to another person's fragment.

Bad:
> “Do the thing now.”

Better:
> “Before sunrise—”
> “seal the north lock”
> “or the glass garden will drown.”

## Consequences

Favor consequences that can be represented by:
- changed location description;
- one new object;
- altered ambient behavior;
- one short follow-up transmission;
- reused dialogue with changed context.

Avoid requiring:
- wholly separate islands per choice;
- dozens of bespoke cinematics;
- mutually exclusive main questlines.

## Mystery

Every mystery reveal should answer one question and create a narrower one.

Bad escalation:
> “The signals are weird. Now they are even weirder.”

Better:
> We learn the broadcasts are not random -> then discover some hybrid sentences predate the sources they were assembled from.

# Visual direction seed

Low-cost but distinctive target:

**painted public-infrastructure diagrams meeting luminous coastal ecology.**

Useful motifs:
- ceramic relay housings;
- enamel warning signs;
- green-black water;
- fog-softened islands;
- practical rain gear;
- colored wire paths;
- glass roots and kelp-like antenna arrays;
- chunky ferry instrumentation;
- paper logs covered in stamps and corrections.

The interface can borrow from maintenance panels without pretending to be a realistic radio simulator.

The world should contain bright, warm accents so “mysterious radio game” does not become visually synonymous with gray static.

---

# Audio direction seed

Audio should communicate source identity and topology, not just decorate the UI.

Possible system:
- each recurring speaker/source gets a tiny interval or timbral fingerprint;
- overlapping sources create audible beating or harmonic tension;
- clean routes separate those motifs spatially;
- phrases the player has caused to migrate can reappear as musical motifs on later islands.

We can do this procedurally with Web Audio in prototypes before committing to bespoke music production.

---

# Scope recommendation

For an actual first company vertical slice, I would **not** build all five islands.

Build:

1. service-boat intro (very short);
2. Glass Kelp Island pre-exploration;
3. one spatial semantic-routing tower puzzle;
4. three aftermath location updates;
5. Tower 12 echo;
6. one recovered artifact appearing on the boat.

That is enough to test:
- place;
- people;
- puzzle;
- authorship;
- consequence;
- persistent mystery;
- desire for a second island.

If that slice does not create curiosity, the larger bible should be discarded rather than defended.
